What motivates you?

The motivation can be very abstract (the “need” to go sailing on a warm and breezy summer afternoon), but it can also be quite concrete (the need to go to the bathroom when your bladder is full). Motivation can be thought of as a driving force on behavior. Motivated behavior is a behavior that is incited to achieve a goal.

Dopamine plays a key role in motivation by acting as a chemical messenger in the brain that influences reward-seeking behavior. When you anticipate a reward or achieve a goal, dopamine levels increase, which reinforces the behavior and encourages you to pursue similar goals in the future. It’s not just about pleasure, dopamine is more about driving you to take action and stay motivated, especially when it comes to pursuing goals or tasks that lead to positive outcomes. In essence, dopamine helps to maintain focus and effort, even when challenges arise.

The hypothalamus also plays a key role in motivating animals to find food, drink, and sex in response to their needs.

You are cold, dehydrated, and depleted of energy. The appropriate humoral and visceromotor responses kick in automatically. You shiver, blood is shunted away from the body surface, urine production is inhibited, body fat reserves are mobilized, and so on. But the fastest and most effective way to correct these disturbances of brain homeostasis is to actively seek or generate warmth by moving, to drink water, and to eat. These are examples of motivated behaviors generated by the somatic motor system, and they are incited to occur by the activity of the lateral hypothalamus.

Avicenna's Wisdom on Health and Malady

My favorite one from a book: Avicenna.

Avicenna (Ibn Sina). Born at the end of the tenth century in what is now modern day Uzbekistan, Avicenna became a profound influence not only on Islamic thinking but also on that of Jews (such as Maimonides) and Christians (such as Thomas Aquinas). If a great thinker is one whose thought can be assimilated and developed by people of very different intellectual traditions, then Avicenna was, without doubt, a great thinker.

Ex nihilo nihilo fit. Nothing comes from nothing, and Avicenna and his philosophy are no exception. Indeed, multiple influences were at work in the formation of his thought. This phrase is often associated with ancient Greek philosopher Parmenides. Parmenides argued that something cannot come from nothing and that change is an illusion, emphasizing the idea that existence is continuous and unchanging. This concept was also discussed by later philosophers, including Aristotle, who examined it in the context of his own metaphysical theories, although he did not use the exact phrase.

Avicenna demonstrated remarkable intellectual abilities from a very young age. By the age of 10, he had memorized the Qur'an and was already studying various subjects, including grammar, literature, and logic. He received his early education from his father and local teachers, who provided him with a strong foundation in various disciplines. Avicenna’s keen interest in medicine became evident early on, and by the age of 16, he was proficient enough to practice medicine and treat patients. His early exposure to Greek philosophical texts, particularly those of Aristotle, played a crucial role in shaping his future contributions to philosophy and science. His extensive studies and rapid mastery of diverse fields laid the groundwork for his later influential works, including the "Canon of Medicine" and "The Book of Healing."

In this post, I want to note his insight on health and malady from page 237-238. Avicenna concludes his general comments on the causes of health and malady by identifying the six most important ones that the patient should consider in preserving his or her health, or the physician must take into account in returning the patient to a state of health. These are:
1) the ambient air,
2) the food and drink one takes in,
3) proper physical activity and rest,
4) psychological activities,
5) getting enough sleep as well as not sleeping too much, and finally
6) evacuation and retention.

These last six factors are what later Galenists would term the “non naturals,” all of which affect a human’s temperament by moderating or altering in some way the primary qualities of hot, cold, wet, and dry. Thus, when one breathes in air, it cools the humors in the heart, and consequently, if the ambient air is excessively cold or hot, it may reduce the natural hot quality of the vital spirits too much or not enough. Moreover, the air might be excessively moist and dry, thus affecting one’s temperamental balance.

Physical activity or resting from physical activity clearly increases the vital heat that is the hallmark of life, but, ironically, this same vital heat breaks down the body and ultimately leads to its corruption, making an adequate amount of rest, which ultimately cools the body, also necessary. Sleep and wakefulness, which closely resemble rest and physical activity, also affect the body’s level of hot or cold: sleep cooling the body, wakefulness heating it.

Psychological activity can also influence one’s temperament, argues Avicenna, particularly when the psychological activity involves remembering some past incidence or imaging some future one, especially when these mental images precipitate some emotional state. Thus, one might remember some particularly embarrassing or acrimonious event in one’s life and feel flushed or hot under the collar, or imagine some frightening scenario such that one’s “blood runs cold.”

Food and drink, Avicenna observes, might have some initial effect when they are first taken in while having an opposite (or at least a different) effect once fully digested. So, for example, wine, in as much as it is a liquid, initially cools one who drinks it, but once it becomes digested it actually heats the blood, at least according the pharmacological theory at Avicenna’s time. Finally, proper retention followed by evacuation ensures that food and drugs that are ingested remain in the body long enough to be fully and properly digested so that the aliment can perform its function as well as seeing that waste material does not adversely affect the flow of the humors and functioning of the organs.

In general, today we do not recognize the underlying causal explanations that ancient and medieval physicians associated with the various necessary factors or causes of health and disease. Despite that, one can still appreciate the overall goal (and frequently the efficacy) of these factors or causes in maintaining health. Indeed, living in a healthy climate, getting proper amounts of sleep, accompanied by a regimen of exercise and a well-balanced diet, as well as positive thinking and the like, are considered even today some of the most important factors in a healthy and well-balanced life.

Rumi The Poet

Jalaluddin Muhammad Rumi (1207–1273) was a Persian poet, scholar, and Sufi mystic. He lived in what is now Afghanistan and later in Konya (Turkey). Rumi is one of the world’s most famous poets because his writings touch the soul. He wrote about love, longing, grief, healing, and the connection between the human heart and the Divine.

His major work, the Masnawi, is often called "the Quran in Persian" by Sufis because of its deep spiritual wisdom. Rumi believed one thing strongly: the heart is the compass leading us back to God.

Adab dan Ilmu?

Etika Utama Sang Pencari Ilmu
Banyak orang bisa menjadi cerdas, namun tidak semua orang bisa menjadi bijak. Perbedaannya terletak pada etika atau adab saat mereka menuntut ilmu. Tanpa etika, ilmu yang didapat seringkali hanya menjadi beban pikiran, bukan penerang jalan.

Berikut adalah 3 pilar utama etika dalam mencari ilmu:

1. Menjaga Niat (Ikhlas)
Segala sesuatu dimulai dari niat. Mencari ilmu sebaiknya didasari keinginan untuk memperbaiki diri dan memberi manfaat bagi orang lain, bukan sekadar untuk menyombongkan diri atau mencari pengakuan (validasi). Niat yang tulus membuat proses belajar yang berat terasa lebih ringan.

2. Memuliakan Guru dan Sumber Ilmu
Ilmu tidak datang dari ruang hampa. Ada perantara di sana, baik itu guru, mentor, atau bahkan buku yang kita baca. Menghargai mereka bukan berarti tunduk buta, melainkan memberikan rasa hormat atas waktu dan dedikasi yang mereka berikan. Dengan menghargai sumbernya, ilmu akan lebih mudah "meresap" dan membawa berkah.

3. Rendah Hati (Humble)
Gelas yang penuh tidak akan bisa diisi air lagi. Begitu juga dengan pikiran. Jika kita merasa sudah tahu segalanya, maka pintu pengetahuan akan tertutup. Seorang pencari ilmu yang sejati selalu merasa "lapar" dan tidak malu untuk bertanya atau mengakui kesalahannya.

Ilmu itu ada tiga tahapan: Tahap pertama membuat sombong, tahap kedua membuat rendah hati, dan tahap ketiga membuat seseorang merasa tidak tahu apa-apa.

Ilmu tanpa etika ibarat api tanpa lentera, ia bisa membakar tapi tidak menerangi. Dengan menjaga adab, ilmu yang kita pelajari tidak hanya tersimpan di kepala, tapi juga tercermin dalam karakter dan perilaku sehari-hari.

Poin-Poin Penting:

1. Larangan Tergesa-gesa
Di awal ayat ini, Allah mengingatkan Nabi agar tidak terburu-buru membaca Al-Qur'an sebelum Jibril selesai menyampaikannya. Secara filosofis, ini mengajarkan kita bahwa dalam mencari ilmu, ketenangan dan kesabaran adalah kunci. Jangan ingin cepat selesai, tapi kejarlah pemahaman.

Cheng Ho

Sang Penjelajah Samudra dari Timur
Cheng Ho (atau Zheng He) adalah salah satu penjelajah paling berpengaruh dalam sejarah dunia. Lahir dengan nama Ma He di Yunnan sekitar tahun 1371, ia tumbuh menjadi seorang laksamana, diplomat, dan kasim kepercayaan Kaisar Yongle dari Dinasti Ming.

Ekspedisi Armada Harta
Antara tahun 1405 hingga 1433, Cheng Ho memimpin tujuh ekspedisi maritim raksasa yang dikenal sebagai “Pelayaran Kapal Harta”. Berbeda dengan penjelajah Barat yang sering kali bertujuan menaklukkan wilayah, misi Cheng Ho lebih difokuskan pada:
Diplomasi, Memperkuat pengaruh politik Dinasti Ming di mata dunia.
Perdagangan, Menukar sutra dan porselen Tiongkok dengan rempah-rempah, gading, dan hewan eksotis.
Keamanan, Menumpas bajak laut yang mengganggu jalur perdagangan di Asia Tenggara.

Jejak di Nusantara
Cheng Ho bukan sekadar tokoh sejarah Tiongkok. Ia memiliki ikatan emosional yang kuat dengan Nusantara. Dalam perjalanannya, ia berulang kali singgah di berbagai tempat seperti Aceh, Palembang, Cirebon, dan Semarang.

Cheng Ho dikenal sebagai sosok yang religius dan toleran. Melalui armadanya, ia turut menyebarkan pengaruh kebudayaan dan membantu dakwah Islam di tanah Jawa. Warisannya masih berdiri kokoh hingga saat ini, salah satunya melalui Klenteng Sam Poo Kong di Semarang yang dibangun untuk menghormati kehadirannya.

Akhir Hayat dan Warisan
Cheng Ho wafat sekitar tahun 1433 dalam perjalanan pulangnya dari Afrika Timur. Meskipun setelah kematiannya Dinasti Ming memilih untuk menutup diri (isolasi), nama Cheng Ho tetap abadi sebagai simbol perdamaian dan pertukaran budaya antar bangsa.

Ia mengarungi samudra bukan untuk menjajah, melainkan untuk merajut persaudaraan di seberang lautan.

Peran sebagai Jembatan Akulturasi
Satu hal yang unik adalah cara Cheng Ho menyebarkan pengaruhnya tanpa menghapus kearifan lokal. Ia memperkenalkan teknologi pertanian, pertukangan, dan pengobatan Tiongkok kepada penduduk lokal yang mayoritas Muslim atau baru mengenal Islam.

Proses ini menciptakan akulturasi budaya yang harmonis. Islam yang dibawa oleh armada Cheng Ho cenderung bersifat damai dan membaur dengan tradisi setempat. Hal inilah yang menyebabkan banyak masjid di pesisir Jawa memiliki arsitektur yang mirip dengan bangunan Tiongkok (seperti atap tumpang atau dominasi warna merah), yang menjadi bukti visual bahwa Islam masuk ke Nusantara melalui jalur persahabatan dan pertukaran ilmu pengetahuan, bukan penaklukan.

Between Heart & Brain:

1. The Heart’s “Second Brain”
It’s already mentioned above. The heart has its own network of neurons, sometimes called the “heart brain”, which allows it to process information and communicate with the brain. It can influence emotional and physical states, even without direct input from the brain. The extent of its autonomy and decision-making abilities is still not fully understood.

2. The Heart’s Connection to Emotions
For centuries, cultures have linked the heart to emotions, especially love and compassion. While modern science confirms that the heart responds to emotional stimuli, the exact mechanisms by which emotions influence the heart are still being studied. The way the heart physically reacts to emotions (such as excitement or stress) through changes in heart rate, rhythm, and blood pressure adds to the mystery.

3. Heart Transplants and Cellular Memory
A strange phenomenon reported by some heart transplant recipients suggests that they may acquire memories or traits of the donor, referred to as cellular memory. There are stories of recipients suddenly developing new interests, tastes, or even emotional changes that resemble the donor’s life. The science behind this is unclear, and it remains a topic of speculation.

4. The Heart’s Unexplained Rhythm
The heart’s natural rhythm is controlled by the sinus node (the heart’s pacemaker), but how the heart develops its precise electrical pattern and why it is so finely tuned remains somewhat mysterious. While scientists understand the basics, there are still gaps in our knowledge about how the heart’s electrical system ensures perfect synchronization and how irregularities can lead to arrhythmias.

5. Heart’s Role in Spirituality
Across many cultures, the heart is seen as the center of the soul and spirit, a source of life and energy. While modern science views the heart as a vital organ for circulating blood, many people continue to view it as the symbolic seat of emotions, connection, and even consciousness. The role of the heart in spiritual and mystical experiences remains an area of philosophical and spiritual interest.

6. The Mystery of Heart Disease
Despite advances in understanding the causes of heart disease, it remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide. The intricate interaction between genetics, environment, lifestyle, and heart disease is still not fully understood. New discoveries continue to be made, but the complexity of how these factors contribute to cardiovascular health is still a mystery in many ways.

7. The Heart’s Endurance and Lifespan
The human heart beats approximately 100,000 times a day without rest, pumping blood throughout the body for an entire lifetime. The exact mechanisms that allow the heart to continue functioning efficiently for decades, despite its immense workload are still a subject of intense study.

8. Test in Life
Keeping the heart “alive” amidst the onslaught of harsh life realities is the greatest challenge as well as the real test in life. The world often offers disappointment, betrayal, and tedious routines, which can gradually make the heart dull, cold, or even numb. However, a living heart does not mean a heart free from wounds, but rather one that remains capable of feeling empathy, maintaining integrity, and finding hope in the midst of failure.
